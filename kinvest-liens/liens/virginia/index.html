Virginia: unpaid property tax and court sales

How an overdue real estate tax bill can end in a sale of the property, in the order it happens.

Read from the law, not from experience. Every point below comes from the Code of Virginia, Title 58.1, Chapter 39, Article 4 (the sale of delinquent tax lands), as shown on the official Virginia Law website on 2026-11-18. Each point links to its section. Local rules (Virginia Beach’s own procedures, rates and sale dates) are not covered. Confirm with the locality. Educational only, not legal advice.

1. The basic idea

In the sections we read, a locality collects delinquent real estate tax by suing in the circuit court to subject the real estate to the tax lien, and the court can order the property sold (§ 58.1-3967). The property itself is sold. These sections do not describe selling tax lien certificates to investors.

2. When a property can be sold

  • When taxes are still delinquent on December 31 following the second anniversary of the date they became due, the real estate may be sold to collect all delinquent taxes (§ 58.1-3965, subsection A).
  • For property with a condemned structure, a nuisance, a derelict building or a blighted designation, the clock is the first anniversary instead (same subsection).
  • For real estate assessed at $100,000 or less, if the court finds taxes delinquent on December 31 following the first anniversary, the property is deemed subject to sale by public auction after proper notice (same subsection).

3. Notice before the case starts

  • At least 30 days before starting the court case, the tax collector must send a notice to the owner’s last known address in the treasurer’s records, and to the property address if different. The notice must say the owner may ask for a payment agreement of up to 72 months (§ 58.1-3965).
  • A list of the real estate to be offered for sale must be published at least once in a local newspaper at least 30 days before the case is started (same section).
  • The cost of publication, attorney fees set by the court and title-search costs are added and are collected if the owner pays to redeem (same section).

4. How an owner can stop it

  • Redeem before the sale date by paying all accumulated taxes, penalties, reasonable attorney fees, interest and costs. A partial payment is not enough and does not stop the case (§ 58.1-3965, subsection B).
  • The tax collector may suspend the sale action under a payment agreement of no more than 72 months, or when someone not in the case claims ownership through inheritance. If the owner defaults, the case can continue without repeating the notices; a second agreement is not allowed for three years after a default (same section, subsections C to E).

5. The court case and the sale

  • The case is a complaint in circuit court. Everyone with an interest must be named: lienholders, people claiming title, and lenders under a deed of trust (§ 58.1-3967).
  • The court can appoint a special commissioner to sell the property and sign the deed. The price at a public auction is evidence of the property’s value when the court decides whether to approve the sale (§ 58.1-3969).
  • The county, city or town may buy at the sale (§ 58.1-3970).

6. What a buyer gets, and the limits

  • Title is governed by the rules for judicial sales generally. It is free of the claims of creditors and others who were made party defendants, but recorded easements from before the sale are not affected (§ 58.1-3967).
  • A party served only by publication may ask the court to rehear the case for good cause, within 90 days after the sale is confirmed (same section).
  • If a parcel was wrongly on the delinquent list and is sold, the buyer is entitled to a refund of the entire amount paid (§ 58.1-3971).

7. Money left over after the sale

The former owner (or heirs and successors) is entitled to the surplus above the taxes, penalties, interest, attorney fees, costs and liens. The claimant must prove entitlement. If no claim is made within two years after the sale is confirmed, the money goes to the locality (§ 58.1-3967).

8. A different route for low-value parcels

For parcels assessed at $125,000 or less where taxes and certain liens are large compared with the assessed value, a locality may ask the court to have a special commissioner deed the property to the locality, its land bank or a designated nonprofit instead of a public auction (§ 58.1-3970.1).

What we did not read

  • Virginia Beach’s own process, sale dates, registration or deposit rules. The city’s website could not be reached from our server.
  • The penalty and interest rates a locality sets on delinquent real estate tax.
  • Other sections of the Code and court decisions that may change how these sections work.

For any real parcel, ask the Virginia Beach Treasurer and City Attorney what applies, and have a Virginia attorney review the title and the court order before you rely on anything.
